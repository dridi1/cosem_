Cereal cultivation in Tunisia is a major agricultural activity shaped by the country's Mediterranean climate, with mild wet winters and hot dry summers. The main cereals grown in Tunisia are durum wheat, soft wheat, barley, and triticale.

Durum Wheat

Durum wheat is the most widely cultivated cereal in Tunisia. It is mainly used for semolina and pasta production, both of which are essential parts of the Tunisian diet. Durum wheat is grown mostly in the northern and central regions of the country, where climate and soil conditions are the most favorable.

  • Karim : Non-hybrid winter variety developed by INRAT, adapted to local conditions.
  • Razzak : Non-hybrid winter variety known for disease resistance and robustness.
  • Khiar : Winter variety well suited to Mediterranean soils and known for high grain quality.
  • Oum Rabia : Non-hybrid variety adapted for winter cultivation and developed to withstand climatic stress.
  • Inrat 69 : Historic variety often chosen for stability and consistent yield.
  • Chili : Disease-resistant variety adapted to winter climates.
  • Mahmoudi : Known for robustness and its ability to produce under difficult conditions.
  • Dhahbi : Spring variety developed by INRAT, resistant and productive.
  • INRAT 100 : High-performance winter variety developed by INRAT.
  • Salim : Variety developed by INRAT and adapted to local conditions.
  • Maâli : Winter variety developed by INRAT for local growing conditions.
  • Nasr : Fixed winter variety selected for robustness and grain quality.

Soft Wheat

Soft wheat is also cultivated, though to a lesser extent than durum wheat. It is mainly used for flour production for bread-making. Northern regions are the most suitable for soft wheat thanks to their higher rainfall.

  • Salambo : Non-hybrid winter variety developed for strong disease resistance.
  • Byrsa : Known for robustness and regular production under winter conditions.
  • Ariana 66 : Non-hybrid winter variety with good grain quality.
  • Vagua : Adapted to harsh winters and offers good disease tolerance.
  • Florence x Aurore : Winter variety developed for grain quality and resistance.
  • Utique : Known for robustness in winter conditions.
  • Tahent : Developed by INRAT for winter hardiness.
  • Haidra : Fixed variety adapted to all seasons.
  • Kods : Winter variety developed by INRAT for local conditions.
  • Jerissa : Modern winter variety developed for high productivity.

Barley

Barley is an important crop in Tunisia and is used for both human and animal consumption. It is especially valued for drought resistance and its ability to grow in less favorable conditions. Barley is often cultivated in the semi-arid zones of central and southern Tunisia.

  • Manel : Developed by INRAT, this variety is resistant and productive.
  • Rihane : Designed for harsh winters with good disease tolerance.
  • Imen : Developed by INRAT and adapted to local conditions.
  • Mercur : Winter variety with good tolerance to climatic stress.
  • Kounouz : Adapted to winter conditions with good grain quality.
  • Rima : Fixed winter variety developed by INRAT.

Triticale

Triticale, a wheat-rye hybrid, is grown in Tunisia for its tolerance to poor soils and difficult climatic conditions. It is mainly used as livestock forage, but also for flour production in some regions.

  • Tritical 18 : Fixed winter variety known for robustness.
  • Tritical 13 : Known for stability in winter conditions.
  • Khir : Developed by INRAT, resistant and productive.
  • Tritical 82 : Fixed winter variety adapted to harsh conditions.
  • Tritical 83 : Known for robustness and grain quality.

Crop Practices

  • Growing Season: Sowing is generally done in autumn (October-November) for harvest in spring (May-June).
  • Irrigation: Most cereal crops in Tunisia are rain-fed, but irrigation is used in some regions to improve yields.
  • Crop Rotations: Rotation is commonly practiced to maintain soil fertility and control diseases and weeds.
  • Varieties: The National Agronomic Research Institute of Tunisia (INRAT) and other institutions regularly introduce improved varieties to raise productivity and disease resistance.

Challenges

  • Climate Conditions: Rainfall variability and drought periods are major challenges for farmers.
  • Yields: Cereal yields in Tunisia are often lower than in more industrialized countries because of climatic limits and traditional farming practices.
  • Modernization: Introducing new technologies and modern farming methods is essential to improve productivity and long-term sustainability.

Cereal Diseases

Durum Wheat and Soft Wheat

  1. Wheat Rust:
    • Yellow rust (Puccinia striiformis): Appears as yellow streaks on leaves.
    • Brown rust (Puccinia recondita): Appears as dark brown pustules on leaves.
    • Black rust (Puccinia graminis): Causes black pustules on stems and leaves.
  2. Powdery mildew (Blumeria graminis): Appears as white powder on leaves, stems, and spikes.
  3. Septoria leaf blotch (Septoria tritici): Caused by Septoria fungi and appears as light to dark brown spots on leaves.
  4. Fusarium (Fusarium spp.): Causes reddish-brown spots on spikes and grains, lowering grain quality.
  5. Bunt (Tilletia spp.): Infected grains become powdery masses of black spores.

Barley

  1. Rhynchosporium scald (Rhynchosporium secalis): Causes oval grayish-brown spots on leaves and leaf sheaths.
  2. Net blotch (Drechslera teres): Appears as spindle-shaped brown lesions on leaves.
  3. Powdery mildew (Blumeria graminis f. sp. hordei): As in wheat, it appears as white powder on leaves.
  4. Dwarf rust (Puccinia hordei): Causes orange-brown pustules on leaves and stems.
  5. Fusarium head blight (Fusarium spp.): Similar to wheat, this disease affects spikes and reduces grain quality.

Triticale

  1. Rusts: Triticale is sensitive to the same rust types as wheat, including yellow, brown, and black rust.
  2. Septoria diseases: Affect leaves and spikes, similar to wheat septoria.
  3. Fusarium: Causes symptoms similar to those observed in wheat and barley, affecting grain quality.

Disease Management

  • Crop Rotation: Avoid monoculture and rotate crops to reduce disease pressure.
  • Resistant Varieties: Use cereal varieties that are resistant to diseases.
  • Seed Treatment: Apply fungicides to seeds to protect seedlings from disease.
  • Fungicides: Use foliar fungicides during the growing season to control infections.
  • Cultural Practices: Use proper spacing, controlled irrigation, and crop-residue management to reduce favorable disease conditions.
